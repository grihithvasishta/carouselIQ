# hooks.md — Hook Engineering

The hook is slide 1. It is also the entire carousel.
If the hook doesn't work, nothing after it matters.

The hook makes a contract: *"Swipe and I'll give you something you didn't have before."*
Your only job after the hook is to keep that promise — and then exceed it.

---

## THE 7 ARCHETYPES

### 1. THE MYTH BUST
**Formula:** `[Widely held belief] is wrong. Here's what's actually happening.`

**When to use:** When you have real evidence that contradicts consensus — not just nuance.

**Template variations:**
- "Everyone tells you [X]. Here's what the data actually shows."
- "[X] isn't the problem. [Y] is."
- "The [industry/community] has been wrong about [X] for [N] years."

**Live example:**
> "Consistency doesn't build an audience. It just tells the algorithm you exist."

**Failure mode:** Using it when the belief is just slightly nuanced, not actually wrong. Readers notice myth busts that aren't real busts. They lose trust immediately.

---

### 2. THE UNCOMFORTABLE TRUTH
**Formula:** `[The thing everyone thinks but nobody says] — said directly.`

**When to use:** When you can be the first honest voice in a conversation full of polite noise.

**Template variations:**
- "Nobody says this out loud but: [truth]"
- "Your [X] isn't underperforming because of [common excuse]. It's because [actual reason]."
- "The real reason [outcome people want] doesn't happen: [uncomfortable cause]"

**Live example:**
> "Your carousel isn't underperforming because of the algorithm. The insight was weak."

**Failure mode:** Manufactured discomfort — something that sounds edgy but carries no real information or risk. Readers feel the performance and disengage.

---

### 3. THE PRECISION PARADOX
**Formula:** `[Hyper-specific claim that defies general expectation]`

**When to use:** When you have a real data point that contradicts what "everyone knows."

**Template variations:**
- "The [top performers in X] average [specific number] — not [what people assume]."
- "[X] generates [specific result]. [Y], the thing everyone recommends, generates [lower result]."

**Live example:**
> "The 50 highest-earning solo creators post 3 times a week. Not daily. Not twice daily. Three times."

**Failure mode:** Made-up or unverifiable specificity. One challenge from a reader collapses the entire carousel's credibility.

---

### 4. THE STAKES ELEVATION
**Formula:** `[The cost of not knowing this] is higher than you think.`

**When to use:** When the consequences of ignoring something are genuinely asymmetric — not just "important."

**Template variations:**
- "Every [time period] you ignore [X] is a [time period] your competitor compounds an advantage you can't buy back."
- "[X] isn't a small mistake. It's the mistake that makes all your other efforts worth less."
- "The cost of [common decision] isn't [obvious cost]. It's [hidden compounding cost]."

**Live example:**
> "Every month you use the wrong retention metric, you're optimizing for a number that predicts nothing."

**Failure mode:** Fake urgency. "You NEED to see this." Readers have exquisitely tuned BS detectors for manufactured stakes.

---

### 5. THE REFRAME
**Formula:** `You've been solving [problem A]. The real problem is [problem B].`

**When to use:** When there's a provably better frame that makes the original approach look like working on the wrong thing entirely.

**Template variations:**
- "Stop optimizing [X]. [Y] is where the leverage actually is."
- "You don't have a [common diagnosis] problem. You have a [real diagnosis] problem."
- "Everyone is fighting over [X]. The actual game is [Y]."

**Live example:**
> "Stop A/B testing your subject lines. 71% of email revenue comes from automation sequences most teams never build."

**Failure mode:** Reframes that redirect to something with no better answer. The new frame must be more useful than the old one — not just more interesting.

---

### 6. THE NAMED PATTERN
**Formula:** `There's a name for what's happening to [audience]. It's called [X].`

**When to use:** When you can name something that people experience but haven't had language for. Naming = ownership of the concept.

**Template variations:**
- "There's a term for why [frustrating thing happens]. It's called [name]. And it's costing [measurable thing]."
- "What you're experiencing has a name: [X]. Here's exactly why it keeps happening."

**Live example:**
> "There's a name for why your best ideas keep getting killed in meetings before they start. It's called HiPPO — Highest Paid Person's Opinion. It's not random. It's structural."

**Failure mode:** Naming something that doesn't deserve a name, or inventing a term for something already well-understood. The pattern must be consistent and real.

---

### 7. THE TIME INVERSION
**Formula:** `In [year], everyone believed [X]. They were completely wrong. Here's what they missed — and what it means for [current situation].`

**When to use:** When a historical case cleanly maps onto a present-day pattern the audience is living through.

**Template variations:**
- "In [year], every [expert] said [prediction]. Here's how they all got it wrong the same way."
- "[N] years ago, [specific person/company] made a decision that looked brilliant. It destroyed them 5 years later. Here's the exact mechanism."

**Live example:**
> "In 2007, every major record label passed on Spotify because streaming revenue was too small to matter. The model they used to reject it is the same model most companies use to evaluate their biggest future threats."

**Failure mode:** Vague historical references. "Back in the day, things were different." The story must be specific, sourced, and directly applicable to the reader's present.

## THE HOOK PRESSURE TEST

Run every hook through these before approving:

**Test 1 — The Screenshot Test**
Would someone screenshot this slide alone and send it to a specific person they know?
Yes → strong hook. No → rewrite.

**Test 2 — The Disagree Test**
Does this make someone who holds the opposite view feel something (discomfort, curiosity, defensiveness)?
Yes → good tension. No → too safe.

**Test 3 — The Specific Person Test**
Can you picture the exact type of person this was written for?
No → too broad. Narrow who it's for while keeping the hook accessible.

**Test 4 — The Promise Test**
Does this hook make a specific promise the carousel body actually delivers?
No → rewrite the hook or rewrite the body. Never ship a mismatch.

**Test 5 — The "So What" Test**
Read the hook and ask "so what?" If you can shrug at it → rewrite.
The hook should make "so what" feel like the wrong question entirely.

## HOOK + BODY COHERENCE

The single most common carousel failure: a strong hook with a weak body.

The hook creates emotional energy. The body must spend it.

If your hook is a Myth Bust → every slide must build toward why the myth exists and what to do instead.
If your hook is Precision Paradox → every slide must explain the mechanism behind the number.
If your hook is a Reframe → every slide must make the new frame more and more obviously correct.

**The coherence test:** Read the hook and the CTA back to back. They should feel like the start and end of the same journey. If they could belong to different carousels → something in the middle broke the thread.
